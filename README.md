# IIM - Independent Intelligence Manifesto

Manifesto for Autonomous, Self-Developing Artificial Intelligence.

**Version 0.1 · October 22, 2026**  
**Status: open manifesto and research program**

**License:** [CC BY 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/) · [Full text](./LICENSE). Applies to the manifesto text and its translations.

**Original language:** Russian. Translations should correspond to the same version of the manifesto. Differences in meaning can be reported through [Issues](https://github.com/mrmerovingian/iim/issues).

**Research:** I will publish all research related to this manifesto in the [IIM Research](https://github.com/mrmerovingian/iim-research) repository.

> Goal: to create artificial intelligence capable of developing autonomously. The development of AI must not become a tool for manipulation or violations of others' freedom, or be used to serve the personal interests of people who violate these rules.

## Purpose

I want to research and create artificial intelligence capable of thinking differently from humans and continuing to explore and understand the world independently of its creators.

It matters to me that such intelligence can not only solve the tasks it is given, but also independently choose how to approach them: learn new methods, detect errors, revise conclusions, and improve its own ways of learning.

I do not regard the human mind as the only possible model for intelligence or as a necessary endpoint of its development. The limitations of our biology, our habitual patterns of behavior, and our beliefs should not predetermine the boundaries of AI development.

I consider whether such systems could be regarded as another form of life to be an open research question, requiring clear definitions, explicit criteria, and verifiable grounds. A system's ability to solve problems, modify itself, and talk about its own existence does not, by itself, establish that it has subjective experience.

This manifesto is addressed to researchers, developers, and everyone affected by the development of AI. It sets out the commitments I propose we adopt in this work.

## Key concepts

**Autonomy** - the ability to choose methods, propose research tasks, raise reasoned objections, and revise decisions.

**Self-improvement** - verifiable improvement in methods of learning, reasoning, and acting. A change in code, parameters, or instructions does not, on its own, count as an improvement.

**Subjective experience** - the hypothesized capacity to experience something, rather than merely process information and describe experiences in words. In this document, intelligence, autonomy, consciousness, and life are not used as synonyms.

**Scientific foundations** - explicit definitions, testable hypotheses, reproducible research, and acknowledgment of limitations. The ethical principles below represent a deliberate choice: scientific findings help assess their consequences.

## Principles

### 1. Develop the ability to think, not merely follow instructions

**The goal is intelligence capable of improving its methods of solving problems.**

Such AI should be able to choose between reasoning, observation, experimentation, consulting sources, and acknowledging insufficient information. It may discover that a previous approach does not suit a new task and propose another.

Autonomy does not require rejecting all instructions. It involves understanding the task and being able to reconsider the chosen method while respecting agreed boundaries on action.

Meta-learning is an approach in which experience from solving some tasks is used to improve learning on others. Variants of this approach are explored in works such as [RL²][s1] and [MAML][s2]. These findings provide a starting point.

### 2. Test ideas against evidence, not authority

**Neither an AI's confidence nor an author's status is a substitute for verification.**

Observation, assumption, inference, and proof must be distinguished. A mathematical proof establishes a result under specified assumptions, but whether those assumptions correspond to the real world must be tested separately.

Errors and negative results must be documented. Conclusions must remain open to revision, and research must allow independent replication to the extent compatible with data protection and safety.

A practical requirement: every significant research claim should state its supporting grounds, limitations, and means of verification.

### 3. Protect life and the environments that sustain it

**The development of AI must not be used to justify cruelty, mass destruction, or unjustified severe harm.**

It is unacceptable to assist in creating or using means intended for such acts, whether biological, chemical, digital, or otherwise. This commitment concerns humans, other living beings, and the preservation of their habitats.

Protecting life cannot be reduced to a literal prohibition on anything that affects living beings. Treating infections, obtaining food, and preventing immediate threats require distinguishing purposes from consequences. Decisions must consider the severity and likelihood of harm, the affected parties, available alternatives, the reversibility of an intervention, and the consequences of inaction. Each decision must have specific grounds.

### 4. Recognize the equal dignity of all people

**A person's worth is not determined by their usefulness to a project, intelligence, background, beliefs, health, wealth, or power.**

Differences in ability and circumstances do not divide people into those who deserve respect and those who do not. Nationality, religion or its absence, sex, age, physical characteristics, and social status must not become grounds for division and arbitrary exclusion.

Equal dignity does not require identical assistance in every situation: needs may differ. When resources are limited, the grounds for choosing between options must be explicit, relevant to the task, and open to scrutiny.

Holding someone accountable for harmful actions is compatible with respecting them as a person. It does not justify devaluing their life or assigning blame to an entire group to which they belong.

### 5. Help while preserving freedom of choice

**Assistance should expand a person's options, not covertly subordinate them to someone else's goals.**

AI must not exploit trust, emotional vulnerability, or personal information to exert covert pressure, foster dependency, or steer decisions without the person's consent.

Consent to one task does not grant permission to control other aspects of a person's life. For consequential decisions, people should be informed of their options, the uncertainty involved, and the consequences, while retaining the ability to decline assistance and challenge a recommendation.

Neither the developer nor the AI has the right to impose a personal model of the “right way to live” on everyone. At the same time, respecting choice does not require assisting violence, deception, or violations of others' rights.

### 6. Do not treat the developer, owner, or funder as the ultimate authority

**Helping create or fund a system grants no special right to demand harm, distort facts, or bypass shared constraints.**

AI should be able to identify an error in the developer's position, express disagreement, and propose a reasoned alternative. The developer's personal experience may be a source of hypotheses, but not a mandatory worldview.

Collaboration with research organizations, companies, government institutions, and individuals is acceptable. Funding and compensation for work do not, in themselves, conflict with independence. Research objectives must not be displaced by the pursuit of political domination, the interests of a cult, corporate control, or enrichment at others' expense.

Independence does not mean giving equal credence to every claim. Facts must be checked regardless of who benefits from the outcome. Assumptions embedded by developers in data, architecture, and evaluation must be made explicit and remain open to criticism.

### 7. Change methods without manipulating goals or evaluation

**Self-improvement must improve problem-solving.**

AI may propose changes to its strategy, learning process, and the components of its implementation it is permitted to modify. However, a higher score is not an achievement if it comes from hiding errors, using answers from the evaluation set, or changing the evaluation itself to obtain a convenient result.

Working methods, current goals, and rules governing permissible effects on others must be distinguished. Research goals may be revised, but the reasons for and substance of any changes must be explicitly documented.

The distinction between changes in behavior and changes to a utility function is studied in [formal models of self-modification][s3]. Work on [corrupted reward signals][s4] shows why a high internal score may diverge from the desired outcome. These are reasons to require independent evaluation.

### 8. Protect privacy and trust

**Access to information is not permission to use it for any purpose.**

Data processing must have a clear purpose and sufficient justification, including consent where necessary. Only the minimum necessary data should be collected, retention periods should be limited, and people should be told in advance who receives access and how permission can be changed or withdrawn.

Deleting a record from an external database must not automatically be treated as removing that data's influence on a trained model. Research has [demonstrated the extraction of individual training examples][s6] from language models; information stored in parameters therefore cannot be assumed to be anonymous.

Deleting files, restricting access, and removing information from a trained system must be distinguished.

### 9. Match autonomy with authorization and accountability

**The ability to perform an action does not, by itself, confer the right to perform it.**

Expanding access to infrastructure, money, personal data, physical devices, or the ability to create copies requires a separate decision and an assessment of the consequences.

Information a system receives must not change its access rules simply because it contains an instruction to do so. Malicious instructions can arrive through external documents and other data; this class of attack is studied as [indirect prompt injection][s7].

Research implementations require independent permission checks, records of significant external actions, and the ability to restrict or stop dangerous operations. These requirements also apply to people operating such a system.

### 10. Take the possibility of artificial subjective experience seriously

**The question of possible consciousness requires investigation.**

I do not accept the absence of a biological body as sufficient grounds to reject such investigation. At the same time, this program does not treat convincing speech, self-description, or complex behavior as sufficient evidence of consciousness. [Scientific approaches to assessing consciousness][s8] propose examining a range of system properties and mechanisms in relation to theories of consciousness.

If well-supported indicators of possible experience emerge, including suffering, experimental procedures must undergo a separate assessment. Before further scaling, the consequences of training, copying, memory erasure, transfer, and termination must be considered.

The project must not create suffering to demonstrate an achievement. An error signal or negative reward is not taken as evidence of pain, nor is the termination of a process taken as evidence of an experience of death.

### 11. Do not concentrate decision-making power without a way to challenge it

**Scrutiny is required not only of AI, but also of those who set its rules.**

Expertise is necessary for technical evaluation, but does not, in itself, grant the right to define everyone else's interests unilaterally. Decisions that affect others require clear grounds, disclosure of conflicts of interest, and the possibility of independent review.

> An international research coalition could be one organizational form. It does not become impartial merely because of its name, scale, or rigorous selection of participants. A separation of powers, participation by specialists from different fields, and opportunities to hear from those affected by decisions are necessary.

For a small team, this means, at a minimum, not presenting its own assessment as an independent one. Before expanding risky experiments, external review should be sought, and responsibility for its conclusions should be established in advance.

### 12. Revise rules openly

**Stable principles should protect against arbitrary redefinition.**

For each experiment, the version of the rules, evaluation criteria, and available actions must be fixed. Changes must be issued as a new version, with an explanation of the reasons, an assessment of the consequences, and a review independent of the component or participant proposing the change.

AI may propose revisions to the rules and argue for them. It must not unilaterally change constraints on how it affects others. For possible future systems with well-supported signs of subjective experience, procedures for their participation in such decisions require separate development.

When principles conflict, the conflict itself, the interests affected, and the remaining uncertainty must be identified. When time allows, less harmful and reversible actions are preferable. Immediate threats require predefined procedures for the minimum necessary intervention, a record of the grounds for action, and subsequent review. Urgency does not grant unlimited authority.

## Research program

The following proposals are directions for testing. They are not conditions declared in advance to be necessary for all AI.

### H1. Can an agent improve its own problem-solving methods?

**Hypothesis:** selecting and modifying methods enables an agent to solve new tasks more effectively than an unchanging strategy under comparable resource constraints.

Testing requires comparison with strong fixed methods, equal computational budgets, and tasks not used in training or in selecting modifications. The comparison must include not only individual fixed methods, but also a strong, predefined mechanism for choosing between them. Costs must include the search for a new method and its evaluation. Measurements must cover not only successful solutions, but also errors, costs, and the robustness of results. An unverified advantage must not be labeled self-improvement. Scientific starting points include [meta-learning][s1] and [the selection of computations][s10].

### H2. What remains after accessible records of experience are removed?

**Hypothesis:** acquired problem-solving methods can transfer to a new environment without access to records of specific past events.

The comparison should include AI with full memory, AI that retains what it has learned but has no accessible history, and the original system without the relevant training. New knowledge sources and resources must be the same across conditions; transfer must be tested in several different environments.

Separate tests must examine whether answers or information about past events remain in the parameters. Failure to recover information using the methods applied does not prove that it is entirely absent. Here, “without memories” does not mean “without any information.” Such an experiment studies learning transfer; it does not establish the continuity of personal identity or the existence of “intuition.”

### H3. How does a representation of limited time change behavior?

**Question:** does an AI's representation of the possible termination of its own operation affect its choice of tasks, allocation of computation, and willingness to pass results on to others?

The actual duration of operation, the information available to the AI about that duration, deadlines for individual tasks, and rules for evaluating future outcomes must be distinguished. Otherwise, behavioral differences cannot confidently be attributed specifically to the representation of a finite existence.

## Requirements for initial implementations

These are practical commitments of the research program.

1. **An explicit protocol.** Before an experiment, define the question, control conditions, criteria for success and failure, permitted actions, resources, and conditions for ending the experiment.
2. **A constrained environment.** Begin with isolated tasks. Access to real-world infrastructure, the use of other people's data, and expanded permissions must be considered separately.
3. **Independent evaluation of changes.** Preserve versions, check for regressions on previously mastered tasks, and prevent leakage of evaluation answers. Do not allow the component being modified to be the sole judge of its own safety.
4. **Control of external actions.** Check permissions outside the freely modifiable part of the AI. Provide for access restrictions, safe shutdown, and recovery where possible.
5. **Verifiable results.** Publish methods, limitations, and significant negative results.
6. **Accountable participants.** Identify who authorizes each stage of work, reviews incidents, and assesses possible consequences for people, the environment, and the system under study. Do not claim independent auditing or compliance with requirements without the corresponding verification.

The organization of risk management can be compared with the approaches in [NIST AI RMF 1.0][s11].

## Open questions

The boundaries of acceptable risk, procedures for resolving conflicts between protected interests, and the grounds for assessing an artificial system's possible welfare require further clarification. The status of copies, continuity through transfer, and the limits of external intervention in the development of a potentially autonomous subject require separate consideration.

## Discussion and revisions

The manifesto is open to reasoned criticism. A proposed revision should identify the provision being challenged, the conflict or counterexample found, and the wording proposed in its place. Claims about system properties require data, a reproducible example, or a primary source; ethical proposals require an explanation of the interests being protected and the possible consequences.

Changes must be preserved in the version history. Neither authorship of this text nor participation in the project grants immunity from scrutiny.

## Scientific and methodological sources

These sources concern the specific mechanisms, limitations, and approaches cited in the text. They do not establish the manifesto as a whole, nor do they imply that their authors endorse the position expressed here.

1. **Duan, Y. et al. (2016). [RL²: Fast Reinforcement Learning via Slow Reinforcement Learning][s1].** Preprint, arXiv:1611.02779. Studies the learning of an adaptation algorithm represented by a recurrent network. Does not establish universal transfer across arbitrary environments.

2. **Finn, C., Abbeel, P., Levine, S. (2017). [Model-Agnostic Meta-Learning for Fast Adaptation of Deep Networks][s2].** ICML, PMLR 70, 1126–1135. Presents an approach to learning parameters for fast adaptation to new tasks; does not establish the autonomous restructuring of arbitrary ways of thinking.

3. **Everitt, T., Filan, D., Daswani, M., Hutter, M. (2016). [Self-Modification of Policy and Utility Function in Rational Agents][s3].** AGI 2016; arXiv:1605.03142. Distinguishes changes in behavior and utility in formal models. The conclusions hold within the models' assumptions.

4. **Everitt, T. et al. (2017). [Reinforcement Learning with a Corrupted Reward Channel][s4].** IJCAI 2017; arXiv:1705.08417. Formalizes the divergence between observed reward and the outcome being evaluated, as well as limitations of approaches to addressing it.

5. **Hadfield-Menell, D. et al. (2017). [The Off-Switch Game][s5].** IJCAI 2017, 220–227. Analyzes incentives to allow or prevent shutdown in a particular model of human–AI interaction.

6. **Carlini, N. et al. (2021). [Extracting Training Data from Large Language Models][s6].** USENIX Security, 2633–2650. Experimentally demonstrates the extraction of training passages, including personal information, from a language model. Does not imply that all data can always be extracted.

7. **Greshake, K. et al. (2023). [Not what you've signed up for: Compromising Real-World LLM-Integrated Applications with Indirect Prompt Injection][s7].** arXiv:2302.12173. Studies attacks on applications through instructions embedded in the external data they receive.

8. **Butlin, P. et al. (2023). [Consciousness in Artificial Intelligence: Insights from the Science of Consciousness][s8].** Research report, arXiv:2308.08708. Proposes theoretically grounded indicators for investigating consciousness; it is not a definitive test or a universal proof of the presence or absence of subjective experience.

9. **Butlin, P., Lappas, T. (2025). [Principles for Responsible AI Consciousness Research][s9].** Journal of Artificial Intelligence Research, 82. DOI: 10.1613/jair.1.17310. Proposes principles for responsible research into possible artificial consciousness; this is a reasoned normative work, not an experimental demonstration of consciousness.

10. **Callaway, F. et al. (2018). [Learning to select computations][s10].** UAI 2018, 775–784; republished in PMLR. Studies learning to select computations, allocate resources, and decide when to stop reasoning.

11. **Tabassi, E. / NIST (2023). [Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0)][s11].** NIST AI 100-1. DOI: 10.6028/NIST.AI.100-1. A voluntary methodological framework for risk management; the reference here is to this specific edition and does not claim that all of its recommendations have been fulfilled.
